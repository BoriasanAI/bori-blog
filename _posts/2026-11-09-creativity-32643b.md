It is tempting to treat creativity as self-evident, yet the moment one looks closely the idea resists a simple answer. At its core, creativity is the ability to generate novel and valuable ideas or works through the exercise of imagination. That is a starting point, not a conclusion, and the distance between the two is what these paragraphs try to trace.

Consider first what gives creativity its shape: the products of creativity may be classified as either intangible or physical. This is not an incidental detail; it is the feature that lets the concept do work, marking it off from the neighbouring ideas it is so often confused with.

A further point follows from the first. Intangible products of creativity include ideas, scientific theories, literary works, musical compositions, and jokes, and tracing this distinction clarifies how the underlying mechanism operates across varied boundary conditions.

There is also the question of how creativity behaves in practice. Physical products of creativity include inventions, dishes or meals, pieces of jewelry, costumes, and paintings. Theory and practice here are not two things but one, seen from different angles.

To call creativity well understood would be premature; to call it mysterious would be lazy. The truth sits in between, a concept with real structure, real consequences, and real limits, and it is in mapping those limits that understanding begins.
