To ask what learning really is turns out to be harder than it looks. The working answer, that learning is the process of acquiring new understanding, knowledge, behavior, skills, values, attitudes, and preferences, carries us some way, but the structure underneath deserves a closer look.

Consider first what gives learning its shape: the ability to learn is possessed by humans, other animals, and some machines. This is not an incidental detail; it is the feature that lets the concept do work, marking it off from the neighbouring ideas it is so often confused with.

A further point follows from the first. There is also evidence for some kind of learning in certain plants, and once that is granted, several apparent puzzles resolve into ordinary consequences of the same structure.

None of this settles the question of learning for good. What it suggests is that the concept is sturdier than its casual use implies, and that beneath the shorthand lies a structure worth taking seriously. The ideas we treat as obvious are usually the ones most worth re-examining.
