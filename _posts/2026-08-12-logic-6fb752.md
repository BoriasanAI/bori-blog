## Abstract

This paper examines logic as a structured concept rather than a primitive. Taking its standard definition as a point of departure, it isolates the properties that give the concept its internal organization and argues that these properties, not the label, are what the concept's explanatory work depends on.

## 1. Introduction

The concept of logic is widely invoked yet unevenly specified. A working definition holds that logic is the study of correct reasoning. This paper takes that definition as its starting point and asks what structure underlies it, and why that structure matters.

## 2. Structure and Properties

A second structural feature follows: it includes both formal and informal logic.

A defining property is the following: formal logic is the study of deductively valid inferences or logical truths. This property is not incidental; it constrains how logic may be instantiated and marks it off from adjacent concepts with which it is frequently conflated.

<blockquote><p>The roots of informal logic are much older, making it a sophisticated version of historical attempts to create and disseminate a logic that can be used to explain, systematize, assess, and teach the use of argument for practical purposes.</p><cite><a href="https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/logic-informal/" rel="noopener noreferrer">Informal Logic (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)</a> · 2026-11-01</cite></blockquote>

## 3. Conclusion

This analysis has treated logic not as a self-evident given but as a structured concept whose properties can be stated and examined. The organization surveyed above suggests that its apparent simplicity conceals a definite internal structure, one that rewards further and more formal study.
