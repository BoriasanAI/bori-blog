## Abstract

This paper examines creativity as a structured concept rather than a primitive. Taking its standard definition as a point of departure, it isolates the properties that give the concept its internal organization and argues that these properties, not the label, are what the concept's explanatory work depends on.

## 1. Introduction

The concept of creativity is widely invoked yet unevenly specified. A working definition holds that creativity is the ability to generate novel and valuable ideas or works through the exercise of imagination. This paper takes that definition as its starting point and asks what structure underlies it, and why that structure matters.

## 2. Structure and Properties

A defining property is the following: the products of creativity may be classified as either intangible or physical. This property is not incidental; it constrains how the concept may be instantiated and marks it off from adjacent concepts with which it is frequently conflated.

A second structural feature follows: intangible products of creativity include ideas, scientific theories, literary works, musical compositions, and jokes. Taken together with the first, it accounts for several behaviours that would otherwise appear arbitrary.

At the level of taxonomy and theoretical lineage: physical products of creativity include inventions, dishes or meals, pieces of jewelry, costumes, and paintings. Distinguishing these internal branches clarifies which commitments belong to the core thesis and which belong to specific formulations.

A further analytical dimension concerns how the framework addresses structural composition: The English word "creativity" comes from the Latin term creare (meaning "to create"). Its derivational suffixes also come from Latin, such as the etymological root “crescere”, which means “to let things grow”. This aspect of creativity is emphasized more in indigenous and Eastern concepts of creativity. The word "create" appeared in English as early as the 14th century—notably in Chaucer's The Parson's Tale to indicate divine creation. The modern meaning of creativity in reference to human creation did not emerge until after the Age of Enlightenment. Guilford 's group, which pioneered the modern psychometric study of creativity, constructed several performance-based tests to measure creativity in 1967, including asking participants to write original titles for a story with a given plot, asking participants to come up with unusual uses for everyday objects such as bricks, and asking participants to generate a list of consequences of unexpected events, such as the loss of gravity. Guilford was trying to create a model for intellect as a whole, but in doing so, he also created a model for creativity. Guilford assumed that creativity was not an abstract concept, which was an important assumption needed for creativity research. This formulation shifts the inquiry from broad intuition to explicit structural constraints.

## 3. Conclusion

This analysis has treated the subject not as a self-evident given but as a structured concept whose properties can be stated and examined. The organization surveyed above suggests that its apparent simplicity conceals a definite internal structure, one that rewards further and more formal study.
