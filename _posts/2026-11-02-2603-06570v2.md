## Abstract

Studying *SUREON: A Benchmark and Vision-Language Model for Surgical Reasoning* (`arXiv:2603.06570v2`), Alejandra Perez, Anita Rau, et al. (12 authors) formulate `cs.CV` state transitions that allocate computation or consolidation capacity selectively under measurable uncertainty. <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2603.06570v2#abstract" aria-label="Source 1: Abstract">[1]</a></sup>

To address this, the authors introduce Sureon, a video question-answering (QA) dataset and benchmark designed to evaluate visually grounded surgical reasoning. The resulting dataset contains 76K training pairs covering 168 procedures and 10 specialties, alongside a benchmark of 340 questions across 10 categories verified by expert surgeons.

Assessed independently, the key strength lies in linking selective compute or memory consolidation directly to testable efficiency-retention frontiers.

## 1. Motivation and Scope: Introduction

Surgeons perceive complex visual cues in the operative scene and constantly interpret their clinical meaning, such as whether a dissection plane is safe or what the current tissue state implies for the next step. As AI systems are increasingly explored towards intraoperative decision support and surgical autonomy, a growing body of work has turned from traditional perception models to Vision Language Models (VLMs) to capture expert-like surgical understanding and reasoning. Yet existing benchmarks fall short of evaluating the visually grounded reasoning that defines surgical expertise, restricting progress on models with such capabilities. <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2603.06570v2#S1" aria-label="Source 2: 1 Introduction">[2]</a></sup>

In the general domain, and increasingly in diagnostic medical fields, evaluation of VLMs has progressed from classification, recognition, and localization tasks to compositional and causal reasoning problems on visual inputs. But unlike radiology and pathology, where image, report pairs already exist, surgical video is recorded ad hoc without paired reports. The field has therefore leaned on rigid expert taxonomies, and the benchmarks built on them inherit those closed label spaces. <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2603.06570v2#S1" aria-label="Source 3: 1 Introduction">[3]</a></sup>

A particularly rich and scalable source of supervision for surgical VLMs is expert-narrated video lectures that are available for training and communication of best practices. Previous work converts these lectures into open-ended instruction data to train generative surgical assistants. Such pipelines scale up data generation by leveraging state-of-the-art (SOTA) Large Language Models (LLMs), but they provide limited verification that benchmark questions indeed require visual evidence. <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2603.06570v2#S1" aria-label="Source 4: 1 Introduction">[4]</a></sup>

## 3. Mechanistic Coupling and Theoretical Properties

Beginning with **SUREON Dataset, Benchmark, and Model**, Alejandra Perez, Anita Rau, et al. (12 authors) formalize the primary process and specify how its core components interact. Moving beyond the closed-space recognition of predefined taxonomies, Sureon tests whether VLMs can reason over actions, states, safety-relevant events, and procedural progression in surgical video. Built from a heterogeneous pool of narrated minimally-invasive laparoscopic and robot-assisted procedures across multiple specialties, it comprises two complementary resources: Sureon -DB, a large and diverse training database of 76,061 automatically pseudo-labeled Video QA pairs for adapting VLMs to surgery, spanning 10 specialties and 168 procedure types, and Sureon, a 340-sample, surgeon-verified benchmark for rigorous assessment. Sureon unifies a broad set of capabilities within a single evaluation framework, organized into 10 categories across two temporal granularities (Figure 2). In short temporal windows, it evaluates recognition of anatomical structures, instruments, instrument-action interactions, entity states, and safety-driven actions, together with locally grounded action reasoning. <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2603.06570v2#S3" aria-label="Source 6: 3 SUREON Dataset, Benchmark, and Model">[6]</a></sup>

Advancing toward **Experiments**, each manuscript links architectural choices to experimental behavior across controlled operating conditions. Experiments on the Sureon benchmark show that (i) no existing model can reliably answer complex questions about surgery, (ii) Sureon measures visual reasoning, not short-cut learning, and (iii) training on Sureon -DB does not lead to overfitting to a specific task, but the analysis shows that Sureon -VLM outperforms existing open-source VLMs on Sureon and has strong performance on traditional classification tasks in surgery. We benchmark commercial general-purpose VLMs (GPT-5.1 (high reasoning), Gemini 3.1 Pro (high reasoning)); open generalist models (Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct and Qwen3-VL-32B-Thinking, Gemma 4, Molmo2); and open medical VLMs (MedGemma 1.5, MedGRPO), and include a human expert baseline. We also evaluate Sureon -VLM, our model fine-tuned on \mathcal{D} _ {\mathrm{all}}, along with the same model trained on Sureon -DB alone or the public surgical corpus alone. <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2603.06570v2#S4" aria-label="Source 7: 4 Experiments">[7]</a></sup>

Within **Conclusions**, Alejandra Perez, Anita Rau, et al. (12 authors) assess scaling evolution, boundary constraints, as well as diagnostic comparisons. This supplementary material provides additional details, results, and discussion supporting the main paper. Appendix A covers limitations and future work; the remaining sections expand on the main paper: Appendix B details the four-stage construction pipeline, including the final dataset configuration (main Sec. 3.1); Appendix C details the public-data training corpus \mathcal{D} _ {\mathrm{pub}} (main Sec. 3.2); Appendix D specifies inference and scoring protocols (main Sec. 4); Appendix E reports the training data-composition and staging ablations (main Sec. 4); and Appendix F discusses ethical considerations. Our refinement and blind-test curation (Sec. B.3, B.4) reduce text-solvability by operating on the answer options. However, text-solvability is partly a property of how a question is framed: a question that names the operative context or telegraphs the procedural phase can leak its answer regardless of the distractors. <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2603.06570v2#S5" aria-label="Source 8: 5 Conclusions">[8]</a></sup>
