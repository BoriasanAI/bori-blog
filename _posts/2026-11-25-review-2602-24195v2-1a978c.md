## Abstract

Examining *Uncertainty Quantification for Multimodal Large Language Models with Incoherence-adjusted Semantic Volume* (`arXiv:2602.24195v2`), Gregory Kang Ruey Lau, Hieu Dao, et al. (4 authors) establish analytic criteria in `cs.AI, cs.CL, cs.CV, cs.LG` separating durable formal lifts from uniform-budget baseline overhead. <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2602.24195v2#abstract" aria-label="Source 1: Abstract">[1]</a></sup>

The investigators introduce UMPIRE, a training-free uncertainty quantification framework for MLLMs that works efficiently across various input and output modalities without external tools, relying only on the models' own internal modality features. We provide theoretical analysis motivating UMPIRE's design, and extensive experiments show that UMPIRE consistently outperforms baseline metrics in error detection and uncertainty calibration across image, audio, and video-text benchmarks, even with small sample budgets.

Tested on matched compute, the formulation exposes the exact boundary phase wherein adaptive allocation outperforms static schedules.

## 1. Motivation and Scope: Introduction

The capabilities of Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have expanded rapidly, going beyond image-text tasks to process diverse input modalities such as audio, video and more. However, reliable deployment of these models in high-stakes practical settings (e.g., medical analysis) remains challenging due to their tendency to produce plausible but erroneous outputs, or confabulations, potentially more so than text-only LLMs given greater complexities of processing multimodal input due to issues like cross-modal grounding failures or multimodal reasoning-access gap. While some works have attempted to directly mitigate these issues during model training by adjusting the training data, model architecture, or training process, these errors cannot be fully eliminated given noisy and ambiguous real-world data. <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2602.24195v2#S1" aria-label="Source 2: 1 Introduction">[2]</a></sup>

A complementary approach to tackling this challenge is developing effective uncertainty quantification methods for MLLMs, which would enable escalation of task instances with unreliable model responses to human experts or larger models. However, existing uncertainty quantification works largely focus on text-only LLM settings that may not capture multimodal coherence signals (e.g., text output ungrounded in the input media), or are designed only for specific modalities (e.g., image-text input) such as by relying on external verifiers, or requiring relatively expensive modality-specific feature engineering that may not be practical especially in settings with resource constraints. Given our inherently multimodal environment, rather than having to specially engineer separate modality-specific uncertainty metrics for new data modalities that may arise, which is not scalable nor effective, we ask: Can we achieve an effective training-free MLLM uncertainty framework that can generalize across modalities without requiring modality-specific engineering? <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2602.24195v2#S1" aria-label="Source 3: 1 Introduction">[3]</a></sup>

The authors show that UMPIRE consistently outperforms baselines in evaluations (e.g., on AUROC, ECE, AURAC) across diverse multimodal input QA benchmarks (e.g., image, audio, video input), and also non-text output tasks (e.g., image, audio generation) despite not needing modality-specific mechanisms (Sec. 4.1, 4.2, 4.3). This includes settings involving blackbox API-access MLLM models via whitebox proxy estimation (Sec. 4.4). <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2602.24195v2#S1" aria-label="Source 4: 1 Introduction">[4]</a></sup>

## 2. Conceptual Architecture: Problem formulation and desiderata

In laying the conceptual foundation, Gregory Kang Ruey Lau, Hieu Dao, et al. (4 authors) separate key operational primitives unambiguously:

- **Problem formulation**: Consider a whitebox MLLM $\mathcal{M}$ that takes in multi-modality input $q$ (e.g. a combination of text and image, audio or video input), and autoregressively produces text output $y=[w_{i}]\_{i=1}^{N}$ that are sequences of tokens $w$ from the MLLM decoder's vocab space 1 We include analysis and empirical results of other modality output such as images and audio in Sec. 4.3. These MLLMs can be represented as model-generated conditional probability distributions $\mathbb{P}\_{\mathcal{M}}(y\lvert q)=\mathbb{P}\_{\mathcal{M}}(w_{1} \rvertq)\mathbb{P}\_{\mathcal{M}}(w_{2}\lvert q,w_{1})\ldots\mathbb{P}\_{\mathcal{M}}(w_{n} \rvertq,w_{1:N-1})$. <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2602.24195v2#S2" aria-label="Source 5: 2 Problem formulation and desiderata">[5]</a></sup>

For each task $\mathcal{T}$, we have task instances $t\mathrel{:=}(q_{t};y_{t}^{\ast})\in\mathcal{T}$ consisting of the multimodal input query $q_{t}$, and ground truth output $y_{t}^{\ast}$ that is only available during evaluation. The authors consider an MLLM's response $\hat{y}\_{t}$ to a task instance $t$ as its most likely output sequence, approximately sampled with low temperature (e.g. $T=0.01$) from $\mathcal{M}$ given query $q_{t}$, and its task instance accuracy as a binary indicator $a(\mathcal{M},t)\mathrel{:=}\mathbb{I}\lbrace \hat{y}\_{t}=y_{t}^{\ast} \rbrace$ that evaluates how well the response matches the ground truth 2 Empirical results are robust to choice of common indicators (Sec. D.3). For image/audio generation tasks without standard binary indicators, the authors use continuous quality metrics (see Sec. 4.3).. <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2602.24195v2#S2" aria-label="Source 6: 2 Problem formulation and desiderata">[6]</a></sup>

Our goal is to develop a framework that computes a task instance-specific uncertainty metric $v(\mathcal{M},t)$ for any $t\in\mathcal{T}$ at inference time that is indicative of task instance accuracy $a(\mathcal{M},t)$. Specifically, we are developing metrics that can be used to indicate confabulations, which is when MLLMs fluently provide arbitrary, wrong responses to queries due to lack of relevant knowledge. Intuitively, the assumption is that the more uncertain the MLLM is about what the correct answer is, the more diverse its sampled (wrong) responses would be. <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2602.24195v2#S2" aria-label="Source 7: 2 Problem formulation and desiderata">[7]</a></sup>

## 3. Mechanistic Coupling and Theoretical Properties

Beginning with **Method**, Gregory Kang Ruey Lau, Hieu Dao, et al. (4 authors) formalize the primary coupling law and specify how its primary components interact. Our proposed framework, UMPIRE, combines several insights to meet the desiderata. First, well-trained MLLMs that can handle multimodal tasks would already possess rich modality-specific features. Given a task instance $t\in\mathcal{T}$, we sample $k$ MLLM responses $\mathcal{Y}\_{t}=[y_{i}]\_{i=1}^{k}$ by standard MLLM inference settings such as temp $T=1$ and nucleus sampling (see ablations in App. D). <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2602.24195v2#S3" aria-label="Source 8: 3 Method">[8]</a></sup>

Advancing into **Experimental results**, the manuscript connects architectural choices to experimental behavior across controlled operating conditions. We empirically evaluate whether UMPIRE satisfies the desiderata (Section 2), primarily conducting experiments for the image-text input setting given its wider range of established benchmarks, but also analyzing audio-text and video-text input modality tasks to assess G1, as well as image and audio generation tasks to assess G1 '. Details of all datasets are in Section C.1.1. The authors use Llava-v1.5-13b, Phi-4-multimodal-instruct, LLaVA-NeXT-Video-7b-hf for image-text, audio-text, and video-text experiments, respectively, and compare UMPIRE against baselines representative of different approaches, including a modality-specific (image) metric Neighborhood Consistency (NC), and text-only LLM uncertainty metrics that we adapt to the multimodal input setting: LN-Entropy (LN-Ent), Semantic Entropy (Sem.Ent), and Eigenscore (Eigen). While NC and Sem.Ent use external tools and hence are not preferred under G3, we continued to evaluate them on other aspects. <sup class="review-citation"><a href="https://arxiv.org/html/2602.24195v2#S4" aria-label="Source 9: 4 Experimental results">[9]</a></sup>
