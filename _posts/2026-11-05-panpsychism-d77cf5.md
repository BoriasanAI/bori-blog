## Abstract

This paper examines panpsychism as a structured concept rather than a primitive. Taking its standard definition as a point of departure, it isolates the properties that give the concept its internal organization and argues that these properties, not the label, are what the concept's explanatory work depends on.

## 1. Introduction

The concept of panpsychism is widely invoked yet unevenly specified. A working definition holds that panpsychism is the view that mind or conscious experience, of some type or other, is a fundamental and ubiquitous feature of reality, including all physical matter. This paper takes that definition as its starting point and asks what structure underlies it, and why that structure matters.

## 2. Structure and Properties

A defining property is the following: it is one of the oldest theories in the philosophy of mind and metaphysics, possibly overlapping in some interpretations with the animism of many indigenous societies, which is traditionally characterized as a more spiritual or religious worldview. This property is not incidental; it constrains how the concept may be instantiated and marks it off from adjacent concepts with which it is frequently conflated.

A second structural feature follows: Integrated information theory (IIT) was first proposed by Giulio Tononi in 2004, and it has undergone two major revisions since then. Tononi approaches consciousness from a scientific perspective, and has expressed frustration with philosophical theories of consciousness for lacking predictive power. Though integral to his theory, he refrains from philosophical terminology such as qualia or the unity of consciousness, instead opting for mathematically precise alternatives like entropy function and information integration. This has allowed Tononi to create a measurement for integrated information, which he calls phi (Phi). He believes consciousness is nothing but integrated information, so Phi measures consciousness. Taken together with the first, it accounts for several behaviours that would otherwise appear arbitrary.

At the level of taxonomy and theoretical lineage: The term panpsychism comes from the Greek pan ("all, everything, whole") and psyche (" soul, mind "). The use of "psyche" is controversial, because it is synonymous with "soul", a term usually taken to refer to something supernatural; more common terms now found in the literature include mind, mental properties, mental aspect, and experience. Panpsychist views are a staple in pre-Socratic Greek philosophy. According to Aristotle, Thales (c. 624 – 545 BCE), the first Greek philosopher, posited a theory which held "that everything is full of gods". Thales believed that magnets demonstrated this. This has been interpreted as a panpsychist doctrine. Distinguishing these internal branches clarifies which commitments belong to the core thesis and which belong to specific formulations.

A further analytical dimension concerns how the framework addresses structural composition: Panprotopsychists believe that higher-order phenomenal properties (such as qualia) are logically entailed by protophenomenal properties, at least in principle. This is similar to how facts about water molecules logically entail facts about water: the lower-level facts are sufficient to explain the higher-order facts, since the former logically entail the latter. It also makes sense of questions about the unity of consciousness relating to the diversity of phenomenal experiences and the deflation of the self. Adherents of panprotopsychism believe that "protophenomenal" facts logically entail consciousness. This formulation shifts the inquiry from broad intuition to explicit structural constraints.

Finally: David Chalmers and Philip Goff have each described panpsychism as an alternative to both materialism and dualism. Chalmers says panpsychism respects the conclusions of both the causal argument against dualism and the conceivability argument for dualism. Goff has argued that panpsychism avoids the disunity of dualism, under which mind and matter are ontologically separate, as well as dualism's problems explaining how mind and matter interact. By contrast, Uwe Meixner argues that panpsychism has dualist forms, which he contrasts to idealist forms. Panpsychism is incompatible with emergentism. This completes the picture of the framework as an organized concept whose parts stand in definite relation to one another.

## 3. Formal Mechanisms and Theoretical Integration

To formalize these intuitions: Starting from the zeroth axiom (experience exists), IIT identifies five essential properties of experience: Intrinsicality (experience is intrinsic: it exists for itself); Information (experience is specific: it is this one); Integration (experience is unitary: it is a whole, irreducible to separate experiences); Exclusion (experience is definite: it is this whole); Composition (experience is structured: it is composed of distinctions and the relations that bind them together, yielding a phenomenal structure that feels the way it feels). By translating qualitative postulates into structural constraints, the framework establishes a rigorous criterion for when an integrated whole exists.

At the formal level: A system is described by its transition probability matrix (TPM), denoted a state-by-state transition probability matrix, over all its possible states. The formalism proceeds in two stages: first identifying the maximally irreducible set of units (the complex), then unfolding its cause–effect structure. This two-stage unfolding links the phenomenological axioms directly to the underlying cause-effect repertoire of the physical substrate.

Furthermore: IIT proposes an explanatory identity: an experience is identical to the cause–effect structure (Phi-structure) unfolded from a complex (maximal substrate) in its current state, such that every property of the experience is accounted for by a corresponding property of the Phi-structure, with no additional ingredients. The identity is meant to be explanatory rather than ontologically reductive: IIT does not claim that experience "arises from" or is "nothing but" a Phi-structure, but that the properties of an experience can be accounted for, in objective and physical terms, by those of a substrate that can be observed and manipulated. Under this explanatory identity, qualitative structure is accounted for by the intrinsic cause-effect geometry rather than by extrinsic functional description.

## 4. Critical Discussion and Empirical Limitations

Despite its formal ambition, the framework faces substantive philosophical and methodological objections: One criticism of panpsychism is that it cannot be empirically tested. A corollary of this criticism is that panpsychism has no predictive power. Tononi and Koch write: "Besides claiming that matter and mind are one thing, has little constructive to say and offers no positive laws explaining how the mind is organized and works". Influential philosopher John Searle has given a critique of the theory saying "The theory implies panpsychism " and "The problem with panpsychism is not that it is false; it does not get up to the level of being false. It is strictly speaking meaningless because no clear notion has been given to the claim. These critiques highlight the tension between axiomatic deduction and classical philosophical intuitions.

From an empirical and computational standpoint: The calculation of even a modestly-sized system's Phi-max is often computationally intractable, so efforts have been made to develop heuristic or proxy measures of integrated information. For example, Masafumi Oizumi and colleagues have developed both Phi-star and geometric integrated information or Phi-G, which are practical approximations for integrated information. These are related to proxy measures developed earlier by Anil Seth and Adam Barrett. However, none of these proxy measures have a mathematically proven relationship to the actual Phi-max value, which complicates the interpretation of analyses that use them. They can give qualitatively different results even for very small systems. Bridging the gap between exact theoretical measures and tractable experimental approximations remains a central methodological challenge.

A further point of contention concerns the boundary between substrate-dependent causation and functional simulation: Because IIT identifies consciousness with a substrate's intrinsic cause–effect power rather than with the functions that substrate performs, it implies that intelligence and consciousness are dissociable: a system might reproduce human behaviour in full while experiencing nothing. The theory therefore stands opposed to computational functionalism, on which carrying out computations of the appropriate kind is necessary and sufficient for consciousness. Neuroscientist Christof Koch, who has helped to develop later versions of the theory, has called IIT "the only really promising fundamental theory of consciousness". This distinction carries direct implications for how artificial and biological systems are evaluated.

## 5. Conclusion

This analysis has treated the subject not as a self-evident given but as a structured concept whose properties can be stated and examined. The organization surveyed above suggests that its apparent simplicity conceals a definite internal structure, one that rewards further and more formal study.
